Why you must not pick the words

A passphrase you compose yourself is not random, and the gap is enormous. People reach for words that go together, names they like, and things in the room — which is exactly the space a cracking tool searches first. Four words you chose are worth far less than four words chosen for you, and there is no way to tell by looking.

That is the whole reason this page generates rather than checks. The numbers below are honest only because the selection above is: every word comes from crypto.getRandomValues, uniformly from a list of exactly 512, with no modulo bias — so each word contributes precisely 9 bits.

If you swap a word for one you prefer, or reorder them so they tell a story, you have taken the randomness back out. Generate again instead.

Where a passphrase is actually the right tool

  • The master password for your password manager. The one you cannot store anywhere, and the one worth making long — in the 2022 LastPass breach, stolen vaults were attacked offline, and weak master passwords were the ones that gave way.
  • Unlocking your laptop and your phone. Typed often, so it has to be memorable; protects everything else, so it has to be strong.
  • The two or three accounts you must reach without the manager. Your main email, usually, and whatever you would need if the laptop were the thing that broke.
  • Not for everything else. For ordinary accounts, let the manager generate something random and never learn it. A passphrase is for the handful you have to carry in your head.
On the numbers. Entropy here is the size of the space the passphrase was drawn from: words × 9 bits, because this list holds 512 words and 2⁹ = 512. It is a property of how the phrase was made, so it stays true whatever an attacker owns. Crack time is not shown, because it depends on how the site stored your password and what hardware is pointed at it — the same phrase can be minutes or centuries, and a confident figure would be invented.

Comparison bars use the same measure and only figures that can be computed exactly: a random 8-character password from the 94 printable ASCII characters is log₂(94⁸) ≈ 52 bits. A human-chosen password is deliberately absent from the chart — its entropy depends on the attacker’s word list and rules, so any bar would be a guess dressed as a measurement. Guidance favouring length over composition rules is in NIST SP 800-63B, which also says verifiers “SHALL NOT impose other composition rules”. Nothing you generate here leaves the page.
