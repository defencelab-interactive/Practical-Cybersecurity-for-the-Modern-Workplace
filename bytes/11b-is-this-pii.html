Is This Personal Data?

Eight things that land on an ordinary desk. Most are personal data — more than people expect — and the ones that are not are not where you would guess.
~2 min
The test
Can a living person be identified from this, directly or indirectly, by you or by anyone else using means they could reasonably use?
Not “does it contain a name”. Indirectly is the word that does the work — and “anyone else” means the question is not only about what you can see.

What the personal-data ones had in common

None of them needed a name. Identification came from a role with one holder, a login pattern, a key held in another file, or three ordinary fields that together leave one person standing.

The practical version: before you treat a file as safe to share, ask who could be picked out of it by someone who already knows a little — a colleague, the client, anyone who can see one more dataset than you can. That is the test the law is describing, and it is also just the sensible question.

Where these answers come from. The definition is GDPR Article 4(1): “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person”, who can be identified “directly or indirectly”. Pseudonymisation is Article 4(5) — data that cannot be attributed to a person “without the use of additional information”, which is why it stays personal data while that information exists.

If you are outside the EU and UK, the boundary moves. Some regimes are narrower, some wider, and a few treat business contact details differently from this. Use these eight as the shape of the question rather than as your jurisdiction’s answer — and if your organisation has its own definition, that is the one your colleagues will be working to.
