Why you were made to do that

Being told how to read a domain changes almost nothing. The two largest workplace studies — 19 789 employees over eight months, and 14 733 over fifteen — found annual training had no measurable effect at all, and that training delivered after a click moved failure rates by roughly one to two percentage points. Doing the reading yourself, on the link in front of you, is the part that works. In a 2 673-person study, people who had to pick the domain out of the address before continuing fell for the link less than half as often as the control group — and on lookalike domains the drop was 79% to 17%.

It costs about seven seconds, and it is the same seven seconds whether the link turns out to be real or not. The tool below does this for you, which is useful when you are already suspicious — but the habit above is the one that catches the link you were not suspicious about.

What stands out
No flags is not the same as safe. This page only reads the address. It knows nothing about who owns the domain, when it was registered, or what the page does once it loads. A brand-new domain built for one campaign can look perfectly ordinary here. If a message is asking you for money, credentials or access, the address is never the whole check — confirm it with the sender on a channel you chose.

Checking without this page

  • Desktop: hover over the link and read the preview in the bottom-left corner of the browser.
  • Phone: press and hold the link until a preview appears, then dismiss it.
  • Read the domain right to left: find the last two parts before the first single slash. That is the site you are visiting.
  • Everything to the left of it is chosen by whoever owns that domain, and can say anything at all.

What this cannot tell you

It reads the address and nothing else. A perfectly ordinary address can still open a page built to rob you: a real site that was broken into, a lookalike domain registered this morning that breaks none of the rules above, or a genuine Google, Dropbox or Microsoft link hosting a document that is the lure. None of those leaves a mark in the address.

So a clean result means the address is honest about where it goes — not that the page is safe. What still decides it: were you expecting this, from this person, through this channel. If the answer is no, the address being tidy changes nothing.

Where the name goes

In dropbox.secure-files.com the site is secure-files.com. In www.dropbox.com/share the site is dropbox.com. Same word, completely different owner — the only thing that changed is which side of the last dot it sits on.

Where the numbers come from.
The inspection-task figures are Lain, Nakatsuka, Kostiainen, Tsudik and Capkun, URL Inspection Tasks: Helping Users Detect Phishing Links in Emails, USENIX Security 2025 (n = 2 673, United States, Germany and Japan). Requiring the reader to identify the domain before continuing took phishing success from 74% to 35%; on lookalike domains it went from 79% to 17%, at a cost of about 7–10 seconds per link. The paper also reports what people get wrong: on legitimate addresses the usual mistake is selecting the whole URL rather than the domain — which is why the drill above asks for the domain specifically.

The workplace figures are Ho, Mirian, Luo et al., Understanding the Efficacy of Phishing Training in Practice, IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy 2025 — a randomised trial of 19 789 employees over eight months, which found no significant association between recent annual training and failure, and an absolute reduction of about 1.7% from training shown after a click; and Lain, Kostiainen and Capkun, Phishing in Organizations (ETH Zurich, 14 733 employees over 15 months), where the group shown training after clicking went on to click more than the control group, not less.

The difference between those two bodies of work is the difference between being told and being made to look. That is the whole argument of this page, and it is the reason the drill comes before the analyser rather than after it.
