The First Four Actions After a Security Incident

Five things that actually happen, and what the same four steps look like in each. The order never changes; what each step means does.
~2 min
1Stop the spread→ 2Tell someone→ 3Write it down→ 4Keep the evidence
Ordered by what each step stops you losing. Telling someone comes second, not last — the hours of silence cost more than the mistake, and you do not need to be certain before you speak.
Which one is happening?

The one thing worth settling before any of this

Who would you tell, and how would you reach them? Not a process document — one name and a phone number. It needs to be a number rather than a chat handle, because the thing that broke may be the laptop or the email account you would normally message from.

If nobody at your work handles this, the answer is still a name: whoever decides, plus the outside numbers that matter — your bank’s, printed on your card, and the client’s if you work inside their systems. Finding those out is a job for a quiet afternoon, not for the afternoon it happens.

Why this order. Isolating the affected machine first, and powering down only when you cannot disconnect it, follows CISA’s ransomware guidance, which warns that powering off loses “potential evidence stored in volatile memory”. Reporting sits second because the NCSC finds that “the stigma of clicking can prevent people reporting it, which then delays the incident response”. Some published mnemonics put documenting before reporting; this one does not, for that reason.

Where personal data may be involved there is often a legal reporting deadline — 72 hours from awareness under the GDPR, and something comparable elsewhere. Your own jurisdiction and your organisation’s process decide the specifics; this card decides nothing for you except the order.
