What changes when you are travelling

The café list still applies — on a train it applies more, because people sit behind you by design. Two things are genuinely different once you are moving.

The device leaves your hands

The one rule worth carrying: devices go in the bag you keep, not the bag you hand over. Checked luggage is out of your sight and out of your control, and the realistic outcome is not an intelligence operation — it is that the bag and everything in it does not arrive.

Hotel rooms are the same question in slower motion. A room safe is a deterrent against an opportunist, not against the staff who can open it, so the calculation is simply whether you would mind losing what is inside.

Borders, kept in proportion

Device searches at a border get talked about far more than they happen. US Customs and Border Protection describes them as rare and puts them at about 0.01% of arriving travellers — roughly one in ten thousand.

Which does not make it nothing, and if you carry client material into a country whose rules you have not read, that is worth a conversation with whoever handles this where you work — before the trip. It is a question about policy and law rather than about settings, and it has a different answer for every organisation.

CBP describes searches as affecting around 0.01% of over 400 million arriving travellers in the last full fiscal year.
What this byte deliberately leaves out. Public WiFi, charging from strange USB sockets, and what to do when a device goes missing each have their own place in the library — the course Laptop Security: Updates, Extensions and Public WiFi, the course Phone Security: Permissions, Charging and a Lost Phone, and the byte When Your Phone Goes Missing. Border search figures: CBP and subsequent reporting of its published data. Those figures are US-specific; other countries publish little or nothing comparable, so treat the proportion as an illustration of scale rather than a number that travels.
