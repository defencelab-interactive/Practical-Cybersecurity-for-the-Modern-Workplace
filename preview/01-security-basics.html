21 February · the lock

Say it is your own laptop. Your documents, your photos, the work you have not finished, all of it locked, and a message asking for money to unlock it.

What does paying actually get you?

Two different problems

Locking the files and copying them are two different problems, and the money addresses only the first. The files were read and copied during the nine days before anything locked, and no payment makes a copy stop existing. Spare copies of everything behave the same way: they get the company running again and do nothing about what has already gone.

Why this company

Wherever you work, somebody is relying on it. Patients, customers, students, or another company that has your employer as its supplier.

If somebody signed in as you tomorrow morning, how far would it reach?

One door, thousands of buildings

Going after one company gets you one company. Going after the one everybody depends on gets you all of them, which is why 15 percent of breaches in Verizon's 2024 report came through a supplier. Nothing exotic opens that door: over ten years of the same report, 31 percent involved a stolen login.

And it is almost never a lapse of attention. Two breaches in three start with a person making a decision that looked reasonable at the time, because software is good at stopping bad code and has no way to stop a reasonable-looking choice.

Your settings

Before the list, one question. Of the accounts you own, which one does the most damage in somebody else's hands?

Your personal email

The bank is the one that feels worst, and it is the one your email can reset. So can the social account, the shopping account and often the work one. Whoever reads your email can ask every other site to send a reset link there, and then read it.

So the list starts there. Each of these takes a few minutes.

This is the setting that was missing in the story, and it is what makes a stolen password not enough on its own.

  1. Open the security settings of the account
  2. Look for 2-Step Verification, Two-factor authentication or Multi-factor authentication, which are three names for the same thing
  3. Turn it on. A code from an app on your phone is the safest, and a code by text message is a reasonable place to start
Start with your main email address. Whoever controls that can reset the password of nearly everything else you own.

Nobody ever said how the password in this story was obtained, so reusing one is not the known cause here. It is still worth doing, because reusing a password is what turns one website's bad day into a problem for every other account that shares it.

  1. Give work email and banking passwords that exist nowhere else
  2. Let a password manager generate and remember them, so you only have to know one
  3. Change any password you have used in more than one place
Many companies already provide a password manager, which is worth asking about before paying for one yourself.

Updates are mostly repairs to holes that are already public knowledge. Postponing one leaves a hole open that somebody has already published directions to.

  1. Find the software update screen in your settings and switch on whatever installs updates automatically
  2. Do the same on your phone, where it is usually under system or general settings
  3. If a device is old enough that it stopped receiving updates at all, it will not get safer from here
The exact path moves with every version, so if the wording on your screen does not match, search your settings for the word update rather than hunting through menus.

A page that wants your password is worth two seconds of looking at. The part that matters is the name immediately before the first single slash, and everything else on the page can be copied perfectly.

  1. Before typing a password, read the address and check that name is the one you meant
  2. If a link arrived in a message, go to the site the way you normally would instead of following it
  3. If the address is shortened, treat it as unreadable, because that is what it is
This is the single habit the rest of this library keeps coming back to, and the one the practice exercises spend the most time on.

Most of the damage after a mistake happens in the hours somebody spends being embarrassed about it. A report in the first ten minutes is a different event from the same report three days later.

  1. Find the email address or number your IT or security team uses
  2. Save it now, and note how to reach them outside working hours
Nobody has ever been disciplined for reporting something that turned out to be nothing. The other way round happens all the time.
Worth doing tonight

Three things worth keeping

A password is a single lock. Once somebody else has a working one it opens the door and raises nothing, because from the inside a correct login looks exactly like the person it belongs to.

A second step is what breaks that. It asks for something the password thief does not have, it takes a minute to switch on, and it was the setting missing here.

Intruders are not in a hurry. The damage came nine days after the way in was found, which is why what is already switched on matters more than how quickly anybody spots something.
